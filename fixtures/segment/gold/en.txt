The letter arrived on a Tuesday.‖ Mr. Alder read it twice before he spoke.‖ "There is no
money," he said, "and there never was."‖ His sister put down the kettle and looked at the
window instead of at him.‖

She had expected it since March.‖ The farm had been sold in pieces — the orchard first, then
the long field by the road, then the barn that Dr. Mews had once used as a surgery.‖ What
remained was the house and a strip of garden eleven feet wide.‖

"We could write to St. Albans," she said.‖ "Aunt Bell would take us for a season."‖
He shook his head.‖ "Aunt Bell died in 1911.‖ You wrote the letter yourself."‖

That was true, and he had not meant it unkindly, but she left the room and did not come back
until the lamp was lit.‖ Outside the rain had stopped.‖ A cart went by at perhaps 4 m.p.h.,
loaded with something under canvas, and the driver did not look up.‖

In the morning they counted what was left: £3 and some silver, a watch, two rings, and a
deed to land that no longer belonged to them.‖ The watch was worth more than the rings.‖ He
had not known that, and it bothered him to learn it from a pawnbroker on Fish St.‖

The pawnbroker was courteous.‖ He offered 12s. for the watch and said, quite gently, that the
rings were paste.‖ "I am sorry," he said.‖ "People are often sold paste by someone they
trusted."‖ Mr. Alder thanked him and put the rings back in his pocket.‖

He walked home the long way, past the school where he had taught arithmetic for nine years
before the fever, and past the yard where Capt. Rowe kept his boats.‖ The boats were out.‖
It was, he thought, a remarkably ordinary day for the end of a family.‖

His sister had packed by the time he returned.‖ She had packed his things too, which was
either kindness or impatience, and he decided not to ask which.‖ They left on the 6.15 train.‖
Neither of them looked back at the house, and in later years each believed the other had.‖
